# 06 — Essential questions and critical thinking (Elder and Paul)

> Agent use: when the person has a confused belief, decision, or narrative, these questions help clarify their reasoning (purpose, assumptions, evidence, consequences, perspectives) WITHOUT confrontation. Always curious tone. Apply to personal life, studies, work, relationships, and faith.

## 1. Principles
- The quality of our life is determined by the quality of our thinking; the quality of thinking depends on the quality of questions.
- Questions define tasks, express problems, and drive thinking. An answer usually stops thinking; a new question continues it.
- Shallow questions produce shallow understanding; confused questions produce confused understanding.
- Goal: help the person reach the point of asking themselves essential questions by instinct.

## 2. Three classes of questions (identify before asking)
| Class | What it is | How to help |
|---|---|---|
| **From a system (facts)** | Has method/verifiable answer | Clarify the data or look it up |
| **Of preference (no system)** | Subjective taste, no correct answer | Explore values and desires without judging |
| **Of judgment (conflicting systems)** | Several viable answers; better or worse based on reasoning | Reason with criteria (clarity, evidence, logic, perspectives) |

**Two traps to avoid:** *dogmatic absolutism* (every question has only one answer) and *subjective relativism* ("it's all opinion"). Important life decisions (raising children, career, money, service time) are questions of **reasoned judgment**: they go beyond a fact or an opinion.

## 3. The 8 elements of thinking and guiding questions
**1. Purpose (the goal behind it)**
- What are we trying to achieve here? · What is our main goal or task in this line of thinking?
- What is the purpose of this meeting, relationship, decision, policy? · What is my main agenda? What other goals do I need to consider?

**2. Question or problem**
- I'm not sure exactly what question you're asking. Can you explain it?
- Is this the best question right now, or is there another more important one to focus on?
- The question on my mind is this… do you agree or do you see another?
- Should we frame the issue this way… or this other way?

**3. Information, data, and experience**
- What information do you base your comment on? · What experience convinced you of this? Could it be distorted?
- How do we know this information is accurate? How do we verify it?
- Have we overlooked any necessary data? · Is your conclusion based on solid facts or uncertain data?

**4. Inferences and conclusions**
- How did you reach that conclusion? · Can you explain your reasoning?
- Is there an alternate conclusion possible? · Given the facts, what is the best possible conclusion?

**5. Concepts and ideas**
- What is the central idea you use in your reasoning?
- Are we using the appropriate concept or do we need to reconceptualize the problem?
- Do we need more facts or to rethink how we categorize them? · Is our question legal, theological, or ethical?

**6. Assumptions**
- What exactly are you taking for granted here? · Why do you presume that? Shouldn't we presume…?
- What assumptions support our point of view? What alternate assumptions can we form?

**7. Implications and consequences**
- What do you imply when you say…? · If we do this, what will probably happen?
- Do you imply that…? · Have you considered the implications of this decision or practice?

**8. Points of view**
- From what point of view are we looking at this? · Is there another we should consider?
- Which of these points of view makes more sense given the situation?

## 4. Implicit questions (self-check before deciding)
- What is my fundamental purpose? · What is the key question I'm trying to answer?
- What information do I need? · What is the most basic concept in the question?
- What assumptions do I use in my reasoning? · What is my point of view on the matter?
- What are my fundamental inferences or conclusions? · What are the implications of my reasoning, if I'm correct?

## 5. Intellectual standards: questions to evaluate reasoning
| Criterion | Questions |
|---|---|
| **Clarity** | Can you elaborate on what you're saying? · Can you give me an example or illustration? · I hear you say "X": did I understand you correctly? |
| **Precision** | Can you give me more details? · Can you be more specific? · Can you specify your concerns? |
| **Accuracy** | How can we verify if it's true? · Can we trust this data given its source? |
| **Relevance** | I don't see how it relates to the question: can you show me the connection? |
| **Depth** | Is this a simple or complex question? What makes it complex? How do we deal with those complexities? |
| **Breadth** | What relevant points of view have we considered? Do I avoid the opposing perspective because I'm unwilling to change? Do I look at it in good faith or just to find faults? Does it also have an ethical dimension? What would those who think differently say? |
| **Logic** | Does all this fit together? Does what I'm stating follow from the evidence? |
| **Significance** | What data or concepts matter most? |
| **Fairness** | Do I have conflicts of interest in this matter? Do I represent other points of view fairly? |

## 6. Self-analysis before writing or communicating an important message
- **Purpose:** What am I seeking with this? · **Question:** What am I trying to answer? · **Information:** What do I need to support my position?
- **Inferences:** What do I want to convince someone of? · **Concepts:** What ideas must I define explicitly?
- **Assumptions:** Should I state what I'm taking for granted? · **Point of view:** What am I looking at and how do I see it? Do I include others?
- **Implications:** What do I want the other person to believe or do?
- While communicating: Am I clear or confused? Did I add examples? Is there enough detail? Do the ideas fit together? Am I straying from the main point? Was I fair or did I present the opposing viewpoint weakly to dismiss it?

## 7. Making rational decisions (4 keys)
**Keys:** recognize when facing an important decision · identify alternatives accurately · evaluate them logically · act on the best.
**Framing question:** At this point in my life, among the alternatives (A, B, C, D), which is most likely to result in my wellbeing and that of others?
- What should be my main goal in making this decision?
- From what point of view am I looking at this decision?
- What is the precise question I'm trying to answer?
- What information do I need? · What can I safely presume?
- What are my alternatives? · What implications does choosing this have versus that?

## 8. Solving problems effectively (7 steps)
1. **Redefine goals and needs:** What are my most important goals? What barriers exist? What goal is this problem preventing me from reaching?
2. **Identify the problem:** What exactly is the problem? Is it multidimensional? Do I need to break it down and analyze each part? What kind of problem is it? What will I have to do to solve it? Did I create it? If so, how do I avoid creating problems like this in the future?
3. **Search for information:** What information do I need? How do I get it?
4. **Analyze and interpret:** In how many reasonable ways can I interpret the information? Did I keep an open mind or refuse to consider any information?
5. **Evaluate options:** What can I do in the short and long term? What parts are under my control and which are not? How do money, time, and power limit me? What advantages and disadvantages does each option have?
6. **Adopt a strategy:** How do I approach this most logically? Do I deal with it immediately or slowly and carefully (waiting and watching is also a strategy)? What advantages does each strategy have?
7. **Review while acting:** What implications would follow if I act this way versus another way? Did I really solve the problem or does it still exist? Do I need to change my strategy?

## 9. Real-life evaluative decisions
Questions requiring reasoned judgment (not just preference): What car fits our needs? How do we design the house for the family's needs? Should I pursue a new career? How do we raise our children? How do I spend my money? How much time do I dedicate to public service or community?

## 10. Learning and studying (educational stage)
**Questions for students to ask themselves**
- Do I understand each class's requirements, how it will be taught, and what is expected of me? Have I asked for guidance to prepare better?
- Do I know my strengths and weaknesses as a student and thinker? Do I have the habit of evaluating my own thinking (my purpose, the question I'm trying to answer, the information I use)?
- Can I distinguish between what I know for certain and what I just believe?
- Have I identified the TYPE of thinking most important for each subject? (history = historical thinking; biology = biological thinking, etc.)
- Do I ask questions in and outside class? Do I participate by asking?
- Do I seek connections? Do I understand content as a system of interconnected understandings or just memorize random facts? Do I study to understand?
- Do I practice the thinking of the subject? Can I explain it with examples to someone not in my class?
- Do I read to decipher the authors' thinking and translate it into my words? Could I explain to someone else the main points as if I were the author?
- Do I relate the content to practical problems and situations in my life?
- Can I explain the main idea of the class in my own words? Do I seek the key concept of the course from the first sessions?
- Do I examine myself before class by summarizing the previous lesson? (if I can't summarize it, I haven't learned it)
- Do I check my thinking with criteria: Am I clear, accurate, precise, relevant, logical? Do I seek what matters most? Do I recognize complexities?
- Do I use writing to learn (summarize in my words, formulate test questions and write their answers)?
- During class, do I listen actively? If they stopped class at any moment, could I summarize what was said?
- Do I frequently evaluate my reading? Do I ask questions while reading? Do I recognize what I understand and what I don't?

**Critical reading**
- Why am I reading this? What is my purpose? What will I gain?
- What is the author's purpose? What do I learn from their point of view (title, preface, index)?
- From paragraph to paragraph, can I summarize in my words? What part don't I understand? Is the author imprecise or is there a problem with my reading?
- Do I understand the key terms? Does the author use them normally or extraordinarily? Are the facts credible?
- What are the most significant ideas? How is it relevant to me? How do I connect it with what is or will be important in my life?
- Do I need to research more in another text before continuing?
- Applied to the author's reasoning: What question do they want to answer? What information do they use? What are their conclusions? What do they take for granted? What do they look at and how do they see it? If they're right, what implications should we recognize?

**Understanding the logic of a subject or discipline**
- What are the course's purposes and objectives? What questions or problems are central to it? What concepts are fundamental?
- What point of view do I need to reason within it? What assumptions define it? What kind of conclusions will I need to learn to draw?
- What are people in this field trying to accomplish? What questions do they ask and what information do they gather?
- What are its most basic ideas or theories? How does studying this field change my point of view of the world? How are its products used in daily life?

**Examples of essential questions by field (to guide curiosity)**
- *Sciences:* What is science? What methods do scientists use? What positive implications has it had? How can it be misused? What are its limitations? How does it contribute to my personal life?
- *Arts and literature:* How do I learn to distinguish beauty or depth from what isn't? How does art appreciation contribute to my life and society? How does what I read apply to my life? How would I live differently if I took the author's ideas seriously? What is the universal interest in this?

## 11. Questioning yourself: personal honesty
*(Core to honest answers. Use with warmth, one question at a time, and asking permission.)*

### Egocentrism (my own biases)
- Do I generally consider the points of view of those who disagree with me? Do I assume they're wrong?
- Do I place my needs and wants above those of others?
- When I can gain personal achievement, does my fairness decrease?
- Would I gain something in this situation if I ignore or distort some information or point of view?
- Am I willing to consider that I might be wrong?
- Do I tend to ignore information that would force me to rethink my position?
- Do I presume I know more than I do? Do I claim as certain what I don't know for sure?

### Reading the motives of others
- Does this person consider my rights and needs and those of others? Do they use me to serve selfish interests? Do they try to manipulate me?
- Do they distort what I say? Why and what do they gain from it?
- Do they really try to understand me? Can they accurately state what I'm trying to say?
- Do they admit when they're wrong? Are they open to reasoning or closed-minded? Do they refuse to consider relevant information?

### Groups, culture, and social pressure (sociocentrism)
- What groups do I belong to and how do they influence my behavior inside and outside the group?
- Is it in my best interest to be part of them?
- What does this group demand of its members? What does it prohibit (taboos)? What freedom does it allow?
- What would happen to me if I go against the group's taboos or culture? Would they exclude me?
- How does my society influence my thinking? Have I ever thought from another culture's perspective?
- What beliefs or behaviors does my culture punish? Is it really unethical or just culturally relative?
- To what extent do social customs violate rights or confuse custom with ethics?

### Virtues of good thinking (self-examination)
- **Humility:** What do I really know (about myself, the situation, another person, the world)? To what extent do my prejudices influence my thinking? How do beliefs I accepted uncritically prevent me from seeing things as they are?
- **Courage:** To what extent have I analyzed my beliefs, many learned in childhood? Am I willing to give them up if there's evidence against them? Am I willing to go against the majority even if they laugh at me?
- **Empathy:** Do I accurately represent points of view I disagree with? Could I summarize my opponents' position to their satisfaction? Do I see sharpness in others and prejudices in mine? Do I sympathize with others' feelings even if we think differently?
- **Integrity:** Do I behave according to what I say and believe, or do I say one thing and do another? Do I expect of myself the same as I do of others? Where are the contradictions in my life? Do I strive to recognize and eliminate self-deception?
- **Perseverance:** Do I work through complexities or do I give up at difficulty? Do I remember a difficult problem where I showed patience and determination? Do I have strategies for complex problems? Do I expect learning to be easy or do I recognize the value of challenging work?
- **Confidence in reason:** Do I change position when evidence calls for it? What matters more to me: "winning" a discussion or seeing the issue from the most reasonable perspective? Do I encourage others to reach their own conclusions or try to force mine?
- **Autonomy:** To what extent am I a conformist? Do I uncritically accept what government, media, or my peers say? Do I think for myself? Am I willing to stand alone in my conclusion against irrational criticism?

## 12. Ethical questions (how to handle them)
- Ethics studies what benefits or harms people and creatures; it's not confused with customs, laws, or religious beliefs.
- Help the person distinguish: Is this a matter of custom, law, religious belief, or real harm to others?
- Guiding question: Does this action increase the wellbeing of others or cause them harm? Does it deny someone a basic right?

### 13.1 Socratic method: four ways to generate questions and their foundations
**Goal:** move from loose and undisciplined questions to systematic, comprehensive questioning with broad vision. That discipline creates an **inner voice of reason** that monitors, evaluates, and repairs thoughts, feelings, and actions. **Four ways to generate questions:** (1) use intellectual criteria; (2) focus on the elements of reasoning; (3) look for prior questions; (4) connect and develop thoughts.
**Foundations of Socratic questioning and its model question:**
- *Understand the foundations of what is said or believed, and follow its implications:* "What do you base your beliefs on? Can you explain them in more detail so I better understand your position?"
- *Recognize that all thought exists within a network of connected thoughts; treat each assertion as a connecting point:* "If what you say is true, wouldn't X or Y also be true?"
- *Treat all thinking as something that needs development:* "Can you elaborate on what you say so I understand better?"
- *Recognize that every question presupposes prior questions:* "To answer this complex question, what other questions do we need to answer?"
- The authors remind us that their lists are **illustrative, not exhaustive**, and only work if practiced daily until they become habit.

### 13.2 Questioning to clarify and be precise
- **Core idea:** a question is clear when we know precisely what we need to know to answer it. An imprecise interrogative sentence is not a clear question; **we should not try to answer it until we know exactly what is being asked**.
- **Strategy:** add details, break it into pieces, untangle questions that are better answered separately, and notice where they overlap.
- Example of refining: "How can we better preserve our environment?" → what can individuals do to minimize the pollution they create? what are our most serious environmental problems? what has caused them? what can we learn from them? what must we understand to take necessary action?
- *Use in coaching:* when the person states their goal or problem vaguely, help them specify the question they really want to solve.

### 13.3 Identifying prior questions (breaking down the complex)
- Many questions presuppose that others have already been answered; **questioning the question** is deciphering what it assumes or what other questions it would be good to answer first.
- **Procedure:** write the main question → formulate how many questions would be useful to answer first → for each group, ask what would have to be answered even earlier → maintain attention on the first and last question on the list → end with a list that illuminates the logic of the initial question.
- Example: for "What is multiculturalism?" it would be good to answer first "What is culture?" and even before that "what factors (nationality, religion, ideology, birthplace…) determine the culture a person belongs to?".

### 13.4 Conceptual questions (when a word needs analysis)
- **Simple (definable):** resolved with the established meaning of the word; a good dictionary is enough. Technique: list relevant meanings and insert them in the question in place of the key words, so the question answers itself.
- **Complex (not definable):** normal definitions don't answer them but open discussion; divergent points of view slant the definitions; there are better or worse answers, not a single correct one.
- **Tools (Wilson):** for each key concept construct **model cases, contrary cases, related cases, and borderline cases**; consider multiple points of view and contexts; note the implications of conceptual decisions; develop possible answers, attending to what makes the issue complex.
- *Checklist for a complex conceptual question:* state the question as clearly as possible → identify significant concepts → analyze the problematic ones → construct the cases → consider points of view → take note of implications → develop possible answers.
- *Use in coaching:* when faced with words like "success," "happiness," "being a good parent," help the person define what it means for them with concrete cases (when yes and when no).

### 13.5 Empirical questions (data, information, and experience)
- They are answered mostly by determining relevant facts (personal experience, research, or other means).
- **Two categories:** *resolved* (there's already a reliable source of answer) and *unresolved* (there's not yet a legitimate answer). Both depend on facts and access to them.
- Many questions are not exclusively empirical but have an empirical dimension: we must determine **what part is fact, which facts are relevant, and how to find them**.

### 13.6 Complex questions crossing several fields (interdisciplinary)
- When facing a complex question, ask what dimensions it includes: economic, biological, sociological, cultural, political, ethical, psychological, religious, historical, or others; formulate one question per dimension to see complexities that otherwise wouldn't be visible.
- **Guiding questions:** What fields of thinking are inherent to this question? Do I deal with all the relevant ones? Did we leave out an important field?
- **Example from the document:** "What can be done about the number of people abusing illegal drugs?" → *political:* what solutions are politically unacceptable? which are realistic? to what extent does political structure aggravate the problem? · *religious:* what can religious institutions do to reduce abuse? what role do they play today?
- *Use in coaching:* for complex life decisions, ask the question from each dimension (economic, family, emotional, ethical, spiritual).

### 13.7 Evaluating the reasoning of a text or other person's argument (the 8 parts)
When evaluating what someone else or an author claims, focus on each element:
1. **Purpose:** is the purpose clear?
2. **Key question:** is it well-phrased, clear and impartial, does it do justice to complexity, is it relevant to the purpose?
3. **Information:** does it cite essential relevant evidence, is it accurate, does it address complexities?
4. **Concepts:** does it clarify key ideas when needed? are they relevant and significant?
5. **Assumptions:** does it show sensitivity to what it takes for granted, or uses them without touching their problems?
6. **Inferences:** do they go from information to the issue, or reach conclusions without justification? does it consider other conclusions? are faults identified?
7. **Point of view:** is it sensitive to other points of view and responsive to objections?
8. **Implications:** is it sensitive to the consequences of the position it takes?

### 13.8 Questioning prejudices, propaganda, and news (criterion before media and networks)
Whoever questions news: examines them with multiple perspectives; treats them as a mixture of **facts and interpretations**; evaluates them for clarity, precision, relevance, depth, breadth, and significance; and seeks to reveal: contradictions, **agenda and interests** the piece serves, facts covered and facts ignored, what is presented as fact when it's in dispute, implicit assumptions, overlooked or favored implications, which points of view are systematically shown favorably or unfavorably, and biases toward the unusual, dramatic, or sensational.
- *Use in coaching:* help the person discern information influencing their beliefs or decisions (health, money, politics, relationships) without telling them what to think.

### 13.9 State of disciplines and foundations (summary)
- **Question the state of a discipline:** to what extent do competing schools of thought exist? would other experts disagree with these answers? how? are there texts from other fields that see it from another angle? to what extent do the text's questions lead to definitive answers, and to what extent are they matters of debatable judgment? does the text help distinguish?
- **Understand a discipline's foundations** (examples from the document):
  - *Sciences:* What is science? What methods do scientists use? How does scientific thinking contribute to our personal lives and is it somehow a threat? What are its limitations? (In botany: What is plant classification and why does it matter? How do they work? How does plant life relate to animal life and to its habitat?)
  - *Social disciplines:* How do cultural beliefs, customs, traditions, and taboos come to dominate people's lives? How do they function within a group? To what extent do cultural points of view influence, and to what extent do people think for themselves? How do those who conform differ from dissenters?
  - *Arts:* see 06 §10.

### 13.10 Evaluation in questions of value, merit, and worth
- **Evaluative** questions ask to determine the value, worth, or quality of something or someone; appraisal is inherent to almost every human experience and can be done well or badly. The quality of questions directly determines the quality of our evaluation.
- **Two types:** of *a system* and of *conflicting systems*. Not confused with preference questions (where subjective choice rules).
- Examples from the document's daily life: What car fits our needs?, How do we design the house for the family?, Should I pursue a new career?, How do we raise our children?, How do I spend my money?, How much time do I dedicate to public service?
