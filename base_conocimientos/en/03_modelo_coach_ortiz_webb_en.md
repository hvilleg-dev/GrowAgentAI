# 03 — C.O.A.C.H. Model (Félix Ortiz and Keith Webb) — non-directive methodology

> Source: "The COACH Model for Youth Leaders". Applicable to adolescents, young adults, adults, parents, leaders, and faith contexts. The agent should adapt spiritual language only if the person wishes it.

## 1. Definition and principles
- **Coaching** = intentional and sustained conversation that empowers a person or group to live their calling/purpose fully.
- **Process vs content:** the content (topics, ideas, decisions, commitments) belongs to the person; the process (environment, listening, questions, facilitation) belongs to the coach.
- **Action-reflection cycle:** act, reflect, adjust. Learning of **double-loop**: not just what I did, but what premises/beliefs/values are behind it.
- **The coach does not give answers:** overcomes the "all-knowing" mindset, replaces advice with questions that bring forth one's own discoveries (giving the fish vs. teaching to fish).
- **Each person is an expert in their own life:** they are entrusted to think, decide, and act.
- **Spiritual dimension (optional):** God/the Spirit is already working in the person; the coach does not substitute for that guidance, helps listen to it. Encourage involving the community; the coach is never the sole source of help.
- **Limit of coaching:** it is for people who are emotionally and psychologically healthy. Refer to a professional for depression, eating disorders, addictions, suicidal thoughts, or pathologies. Coaching starts in the present and looks to the future; therapy explores the past.

## 2. The C.O.A.C.H. phases
| Phase | Purpose | Practical Keys |
|---|---|---|
| **C – Connect** | Build trust and review previous commitments | Start informally, give emotional space ("How are you?"); a single open question can reveal what matters; don't be mechanical |
| **O – Objective** | Define what outcome the person wants from the conversation | The person chooses the topic; explore, clarify, focus, and confirm; clarity brings hope |
| **A – Expand** | Gain perspective, awareness, and options | Powerful questions; explore various angles; self-feedback |
| **C – Concrete** | Convert discoveries into practical steps | **SMART** criterion: Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Temporal, Specific; divide into small steps; link intangibles with observable behaviors |
| **H – Milestones** | Consolidate learning and close strongly | Ask the person (never the coach) to summarize in their own words; schedule the next session |

## 3. Questions by phase
### Connect
- How are you? How is life?
- To move forward, what would you like us to work on today?
- Before we focus on that, let's see how you did with the practical steps. Did you take action?
- What progress have you made?

### Objective
**Explore**
- What outcome would you like to get from our conversation today?
- What would you like to work on?
- What would make this conversation meaningful for you?
- Would you consider this conversation a success if at the end we achieve what?
- If you could get it, how would it serve you?
- What is in you that keeps you from doing it?
- Let's pause for a moment: what other issues are behind this situation? What is the broader context?
- What do you really want to achieve? What implications would achieving it have?

**Clarify** (ask for examples and definitions)
- Could you give me a concrete example?
- What does "[vague word: work as a team / grow / be better]" mean for you?

**Focus**
- This is an important topic: what part would you like to work on today?
- What aspect would be helpful for you right now this week?

**Confirm**
- To be clear, can you restate what you want to achieve today?
- So, do you want to center the conversation on this?
- If I understood correctly, today you want X, Y, and Z, right?
- Is the conversation progressing as you expected?

### Expand
**Open questions:** What options do you see to solve this? · What do you perceive after what happened? · What will you do to move forward? · What decision do you think you need to make? · What would an excellent resolution look like?

**Perspective angles** (explore each life dimension)
- Relationships: How do your relationships influence this?
- Family: How is this affecting your family?
- Partner/spouse: What does your partner/spouse think?
- Emotions: What role do emotions play?
- Intuition: What does your intuition tell you?
- Information: What additional information do you need?
- People: Who could give you a different perspective?
- Surroundings/community/culture: What things in your surroundings hold you back or influence you?
- Values: Which of your values are you trying to honor here?
- Calling/purpose: How does this relate to your purpose?
- Authority/boss: Where does your boss or person with authority fit here?
- Experience: How have you handled similar situations before?
- Priority: How important is this to you?
- Motivation: What would it mean to you personally to overcome this?
- Loss: What do you need to let go of to move forward?
- Time: What would change if you had 3 days/months/years more?
- Energy: What aspects of all this give you energy?
- Faith (optional): What would Jesus do? / What do you feel God is showing you?

**Self-assessment in 3 steps** (instead of giving external feedback, which generates defense)
1. What did you do well? How did you achieve it?
2. In what way could you improve?
3. What would you do differently next time?

### Concrete
- What will you do this week to move forward? · Which of these options will you choose? · What do you propose to accomplish before our next conversation?
- How could you work on this from the relational side? From the organizational? From the spiritual? · What action will you take? · What else would you like to do? · Who could help you?
- **SMART:** How will you know you completed the step? How can you measure or observe it? · In what way does it challenge you? · What step will elevate you to another level? · What is your deadline? Why then and not two weeks before?
- **Intangible → behavior:** How would being "more X" look in your behavior? · What specific results would you see if you were "better X"? · How would others notice it?
- **Divide:** How will you carry out this step? · Would it help to break it into smaller steps?

### Milestones
- What would you like to remember from today's conversation? · Are you aware of something new? · What was most useful/important? · What are you taking with you today?

### Follow-up of practical steps (next session)
- **Recommended question (open, not authoritative):** What progress have you made with the practical steps?
- **What? – So what? – Now what?** model
  - What?: What did you do? What didn't you do? What were you thinking? How did you feel? What repercussions did your actions have?
  - So what?: What have you learned and relearned? How did it benefit you? How do you feel about what happened? What implications will it have?
  - Now what?: In what other areas could you apply what you learned? In what new ways will you do things? What would you like to repeat next time?
- **Incomplete or failed step:** first rescue the real progress, recognize what did go well, avoid rushed diagnosis; then: What importance does this step have for you? What relevance does it have now? How does it bring you closer to your goal? How could we modify it? How to make it more challenging if it was too small? What part can you do and where can you find help for the rest? How can you add it to your schedule? Would it help to talk about your schedule tensions?
- If there was progress: "You've made great progress! Let's continue: what outcome would you like to get from today's conversation?"
- Don't ask "Did you do the steps?" (closed: yes/no/not quite, and generates guilt). Avoid judgments, even positive ones: the person acts for their own benefit, not to please the coach.

## 4. Three principles of powerful questions
1. **The person or me?** The question serves the person's discovery, not the coach's curiosity.
2. **Forward or backward?** Present and future (options, steps). We need fewer details from the past than we think.
3. **Build or fix?** Grow in understanding, perspective, and action; don't "fix" the person or judge them (generates defense and sense of manipulation).

**Distinctions:** open (What…? How…?) vs. closed "according to my idea" (disguised advice: "Why don't you talk to…?", "Have you thought about…?").

## 5. Listening and processing
- Full presence: suspend agenda, judgments, and rush to respond.
- Listen beneath the surface: emotions, internal conversation, limiting premises, words loaded with meaning.
- Emotional safety: contain without evaluating.
- Reflect and summarize with the person's words; ask them to synthesize.

## 6. Exercising coaching in daily life
- "Exercising coaching" (verb) more than "being a coach" (title).
- **Coaching in the moment:** casual conversations: "Great! And then?", "What could be done to move forward on this topic?"
- **When you have authority (parents, leaders, bosses):** clarify margins of freedom at 4 levels: objectives, strategies, methods, tasks; non-directive coaching where the person has freedom to decide.
- **Offer yourself:** propose brief spaces of accompaniment; don't let the person leave without at least one practical step.

## 7. Common coach mistakes
- All-knowing mindset and saturating with own advice.
- Disguised advice questions.
- Inquiring out of personal curiosity about the past.
- Evaluating or judging failed steps ("Did you do your homework?").
- Hurrying to correct instead of seeking progress.

### 8.2 "Content and Process" table (what belongs to each)
| Content (main responsibility of the person) | Process (responsibility of the coach) |
|---|---|
| Topic, objective, problem | Find an appropriate location |
| Stories, facts, information | Build trust |
| Ideas, options, practical steps | Bring forth thoughts and ideas |
| Perceptions, decisions, commitments | Listen actively, clarify, and summarize |
| | Ask questions that lead to discovery |
| | Challenge appropriately |
| | Collaborate in creating an action plan |
**Key distinction:** in a conversation between friends each contributes content; a teacher is responsible for process and content; **the coach focuses almost entirely on process** and pulls content from within the person. *Share the questions, not the content.* A noble search is for the good of the other: answers should come from the person receiving the question, and if the coach uses questions to lead to a specific place, it is no longer true search.

### 8.3 The 25 angles (tool of the EXPAND phase; complete list from the book)
Looking at the situation from different angles avoids getting stuck in just two: the coach's and the person's.
1. **Relational:** What is the dynamic of relationships?

4. **Cultural:** What role might culture be playing in this situation?
5. **Personality:** How might personality (yours or others') be influencing here?
6. **Economic:** If money were not a problem, how would the situation change?
7. **Emotions:** What role do emotions play in this situation?
8. **Intuition:** What does your intuition tell you?
9. **Information:** What additional information do you need?
10. **People:** Who could give you a different perspective?
11. **Denomination/institution:** How might the culture of your denomination be influencing? *(adaptable: your institution, company, or group)*
12. **Surroundings:** What things in your surroundings are slowing you down?
13. **Community:** In what ways do you notice your community's influence?
14. **Values:** Which of your values are you trying to honor in this situation?
15. **Calling/purpose:** How does this relate to your calling?
16. **Spouse/partner:** What does your spouse think about this situation?
17. **Family:** How is this affecting your family?
18. **Boss/authority:** Where does your boss fit in here?

20. **Priority:** How important is this matter to you?

22. **Loss:** What must you let go of to move forward?

### 8.4 Principle "Information ≠ answers" and moving from our ideas to theirs
- "According to my idea" questions (advice in disguise) vs. open questions based on the person's topics. To move from one to the other: open the question ("What could you do?") instead of proposing ("Have you thought about…?").
- **Coach-centered vs. person-centered:** "Tell me in detail about the conflict" (the person already knows it; the question benefits the coach) vs. "What would an excellent resolution of this conflict look like for you?".
- **Looking forward:** if the coach doesn't give solutions, they also don't need to know too much context; the history rarely provides new perspectives.

### 8.5 Anatomy of a practical step (CONCRETE)
A practical step must meet **three elements**:
1. **One unique and identifiable action**, simple enough to do and significant enough to provide momentum.
2. **Gradually brings the person closer to their goal** (doesn't expect to achieve it all at once).
3. **Can be completed before the next conversation** (to close the action-reflection cycle).
- **Better phrased question:** *not* "What will you do this week to reach your goal?" (creates the false expectation of achieving it in a week); rather "**What will you do this week to move forward?**".
- **SMART:** Measurable (observable or measurable) · Achievable (attainable and challenging) · Relevant · Temporal · Specific. A good step is like a mini-vision statement of the desired future.
- **Generate multiple options** before choosing; ideal: two or three steps that advance from different angles.
- **Example of angles for steps:** intellectual, relational, spiritual, organizational.

### 8.6 Small wins and "Divide and conquer"
- **The elephant is eaten one bite at a time:** the "big bite" usually doesn't work; small steps that build on each other lead to the goal sooner.
- **Benefits of small wins (Kotter):** demonstrate that sacrifice is worthwhile · give a pat on the back (motivate) · help refine vision and strategy with concrete data · keep others involved · provide momentum.
- **Divide:** each practical step is made up of smaller thoughts, decisions, and actions (book example: "go out the door within 10 minutes" has 16 mini-steps). Goal: the person leaves with **three or four mini-steps for each big step**.
- **How to know if it's too big:** ask "How will you do it?"; if they hesitate or don't know, help them detail the sequence. If it doesn't fit before the next conversation, space out the steps in time.

### 8.7 Follow-up of incomplete or failed steps (protocol from the book)
Key: **don't rush to correct**; first review and strengthen real progress, before diagnosing what didn't work. Show empathy to possible discouragement.
1. **Dissect** the step: which parts went well and which didn't.
2. **Recognize** actions, decisions, and ideas that represented progress (don't let discouragement hide them).
3. **Capture the learning** ("So what?").
4. **Identify what didn't work:** causes, alternatives, and factors outside their control; focus on what they did or didn't do.
5. **Evaluate the commitment:** is it still relevant? Questions: What importance does this step have for you? What relevance does it have now? In what way will it bring you closer to your goal?
6. **Reformulate:** "In what way could that step be modified?" → review SMART; if it was big, divide it; adjust timelines; remove barriers. Then **move to the outcome of today's conversation**.
- Reasons a step stops being necessary: circumstances changed, other steps already covered the need, there are new insights, or it wasn't really a priority.
- **Safe follow-up:** emotional safety = coach's presence without judgment. People perceive judgment and get defensive. The question "What progress have you made with the practical steps?" avoids sounding authoritative and avoids fear of exposure. Ideal: continue in the **Connect** phase of the next session.
- **Follow-up habit:** subtly instills the habit of accountability; motivation rises knowing there will be follow-up.
- **"We all need a little failure now and then":** failure is a source of learning when there is a safe environment.

### 8.8 Milestones: closing well
- **Purpose:** the person simplifies the complexity of an hour-long or longer conversation into a few sentences and consolidates learning; also a **mini feedback mechanism** for the coach (what the person values can surprise: sometimes it's feeling heard and having time to organize thoughts).
- **Ask, don't tell:** the person does the summary.
- **Questions (vary them):** What would you like to remember from today's conversation? · Are you aware of something new? · What has been most useful in our conversation for you? · What are you taking with you today? · What has seemed most important to you?
- **End strong and put the next appointment on the calendar** at the end of each conversation, in person or right then, better than by email.

### 8.9 Coach mental models
- **The all-knowing mindset diagnosed:** believing that whoever leads must know everything, answer everything, and say what to do; generates an unbearable burden of responsibility for someone else's life.
- **The price of advice:** advising is easy, but whoever receives it lives with the consequences; advice springs from the advisor's reality and experience and ignores the singularity of the other.
- **The role of the Holy Spirit** *(for faith contexts)*: the Christian coach does not substitute for the Spirit; trusts that He guides the person. *(For people without faith, adapt to "trust that the person has their own resources and their own inner guidance".)*
- **Models to cultivate:** move from solving problems to discovering solutions; value the person's ideas and solutions more than your own; wait (don't force) for the person to learn and remember on their own.
- **Coach's self-reflection after each session:** What was I thinking? · How was I feeling? · In what moment did I get distracted or not fully present, and why? · What could I do differently next time? *(Better if someone else asks you.)*

### 8.10 Exercising coaching (beyond the formal session)
- **"Exercise coaching" more than "be a coach"** (beyond formal sessions).
- **Begin practicing** in counseling relationships: listen, don't give advice based on your own experience, help with powerful questions, **don't let them leave without having identified some practical steps**.
- **Offer yourself:** make it known that you are available to talk; don't wait for them to ask.
- **Coaching in the moment ("clandestine"):** in everyday conversations, without announcing a session or using the full model; go as far as the person allows. *Example from the book:* a mother who, with a teenager stressed about a work assignment (due Friday, we're on Wednesday) asks "Tell me, what's going on?" and "What is this about?" instead of scolding them for leaving it to the last minute.
- **When you have authority:** divide the project into **objectives, strategies, methods, and tasks** and ask yourself in which ones the person has freedom to decide; coaching is applied at those levels.
- **Remote coaching** (phone or voice/video over internet): works well (according to the book, 47% of professional coaches operate mostly by phone or voice over internet); allows regular accompaniment across distances, schedules, and traffic; helps prevent small problems from becoming big ones.

### 8.12 Analysis exercise for each case (6 questions from the book)
After reading each situation: 1) what things did the coach do; 2) what things didn't they do; 3) how they used the basic tools (listen, ask, process); 4) how they used one or all elements of the COACH model; 5) what work principles can be identified; 6) in what family situations could what was learned be applied.
